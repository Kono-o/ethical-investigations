This paper is about investigating ***morality***.
It is written for anyone to pick up and read.

Philosophy has a reputation for being difficult, abstract, and pompous. Some of it is. Therefore, it has been my goal to write this paper such that no prior knowledge of philosophy or science is assumed or expected of the reader, because every idea that matters within it will be introduced and explained before it is used.

If there is a spirit in which this paper hopes to be read, it is one of open curiosity. My only request is that you (the reader) sit with its contents with a sense of honest inquiry, and follow the arguments where they lead. Whether you agree with the conclusion or not is entirely up to you.

In fact, the conclusion it reaches is probably an unfamiliar one, and it arrives there slowly, through the investigative territory it covers. The hope is that by the time it does, it will feel earned rather than imposed.
By the end, whatever you do take away,I hope you will feel that the questions were worth pondering, and that the world looks a little clearer to you for having considered them carefully. 

I draw on several of branches philosophy, evolutionary biology, psychology, neuroscience, sociology, and a few miscellaneous other fields, to draft the arguments and areas of exploration within this paper. It is done not to show off range, but because ethics is a phenomenon that cannot be understood from within any single discipline. I have tried to distil down concepts and ideas, each with their own complexity in the fields they originate from into their most essential, pure and simply understood forms in order for the paper to be relatively short and easy to follow. As a result, there can be some loss of nuance, but I have tried to mitigate them to the best of my abilities.

I have also refrained from using vocabulary that is too uncommon, unless absolutely necessary. Any term that has a strict/different meaning within their respective field, (for example, the term ***phenomenon*** has a very different meaning in the context of philosophy, compared to regular speech), has been made ***italic***, like this ***word***. On the first occurrence of such words, they are clearly defined. Any word that is not ***italicised***, you are safe to assume them to mean what they mean in normal everyday speech.

Now, a few clarifications and definitions that will make the rest of the paper easier to read:

1. When I use the words ***morality***, ***morals***, and ***values***, I mean the same thing, which is the domain of our lives that are concerned with good and bad, right and wrong, what we should do and what we shouldn't do, what we owe each other. These words are sometimes distinguished in technical philosophical writing, but that distinction will not matter here. Therefore these three words will be used interchangeably throughout. What matters is that they all point at the same thing, which is the part of human experience that involves moral judgement, moral feeling, and moral belief.

2. ***Ethics***, refers to the philosophical study of ***morality***. It is the systematic attempt to understand what ***morality*** is, where it comes from, and whether moral claims can be true or false. In everyday speech, ***ethics***and ***morality*** are used interchangeably, but for our purposes, the distinction matters. ***Morality*** is the thing itself, ***ethics*** is the study of it. When the word ***ethics*** appears, it will mean this.

3. The distinction of ***descriptive*** and ***prescriptive*** matters enormously throughout this paper. A ***descriptive*** statement says something about how things are. "The cat is sleeping on the bed," "The bus is yellow, "The Earth orbits the Sun" are descriptions of certain things within the world, or that those things are in a certain way. A ***prescriptive*** statement says something about how things should be. "You should pass the salt," "I must buy new shoes," "It is important to study", are ***prescriptions***. They prescribe some form of state, that the world ***ought*** to be in.

4. A statement is ***truth-apt*** if it is the kind of statement that can be true or false, that is, if it is the kind of statement where the question "is that true?" even makes sense to ask. "The Earth orbits the Sun" is ***truth-apt***, the factual claim of Earth orbiting the Sun could be true or false, and we can in principle find out if it is. "Pass the salt" is not ***truth-apt***. It is a command. Asking whether it is true or false doesn't make sense. It is a ***category error***, meaning a fallacy where an object, property, or concept is mistakenly assigned to a category it does not belong to. Similarly, "Ouch!" is not ***truth-apt*** either. It is an expression of pain. It doesn't describe anything, it just expresses something. If the statement was "I just felt pain", then it could be ***truth-apt***,for it could be true or false whether you did feel pain.

5. A ***moral*** or ***ethical*** ***statement***, refers to any sentence that says something is good or bad, right or wrong, that something should or shouldn't be done. "Lying is wrong," "You should keep your promises," "That was a cruel thing to do", these are all ***moral statements***. Now, an interesting aspect of such statements are that, its hard to tell if they are ***descriptive*** or ***prescriptive***. "Lying is wrong" has the grammatical form of a ***descriptive statement***. It looks like it is saying something about the act of lying, the way "The bus is yellow" says something about the bus. But it also carries a prescriptive force, it seems to be saying something about what we should or shouldn't do. Whether ***moral statements*** are ultimately ***descriptive***, ***prescriptive***, or something else entirely is one of the central questions this paper works toward. Whether they are ***truth-apt*** also remains to be seen. For now, we can simply note that they seem to look and behave like assertions, we (as humans) do often treat them as things that can be true or false, we argue about them, we disagree about them all the time,and we call each other wrong. That much is enough to proceed.

With those clarifications in place, we can begin. The first question is also the most natural one, the one anyone would ask if they were starting from scratch. What actually is morality? Not philosophically, not technically, but as a real thing that real people carry around with them every day. That is where we start.
