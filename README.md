This paper is a sustained philosophical investigation into the nature of morality: what it is, where it comes from, and whether it corresponds to anything real in the world. It is written for anyone willing to think carefully, requiring no prior knowledge of philosophy, because it builds every foundation it stands on from within itself. It moves through the whole of moral philosophy and meta-ethics, from the ordinary person's intuitive sense that some things are just wrong, through the great ethical traditions, through the sciences of mind and evolution, through logic and language, not as a neutral survey but as a deliberate and cumulative argument.

The argument it makes is this: that moral facts do not exist. Not that morality is unimportant to people, not that moral experience is illusory, not that societies don't need moral frameworks, all of that is granted freely. The claim is strictly ontological: that when moral statements assert the existence of mind-independent moral facts, facts that would be true regardless of what any human being or culture thinks or feels, they are making a claim that is false. There is nothing in the fabric of the world that corresponds to "wrong" or "good" or "ought" in the way moral discourse assumes. This is Mackian error theory, and it is the conclusion the paper argues for.

Crucially, this is a descriptive claim about the nature of reality, not a prescriptive claim about how to live. It is not pop nihilism, it does not say nothing matters, or that cruelty is permissible, or that moral discourse should be abandoned. It says only what is true about what morality is: a deeply human phenomenon, rooted in evolution, psychology, culture, and language, that systematically presents itself as something more than it is. The investigation treats that phenomenon with the seriousness it deserves, which is precisely why it ends where it ends.
